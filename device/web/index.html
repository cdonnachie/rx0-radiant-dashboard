Absolute GH/s is the number to compare clocks on. The percentage divides by a figure that scales with the clock, so lowering the clock on a board that has plateaued raises the percentage while the machine does less work. Use it to reject a clock that is clearly failing — both figures fall together then — not to choose between two that work.

Hashrate is a five-minute trailing average (the miner's rtpow), not an instantaneous reading. Two samples a minute apart share four fifths of their window, so repeating a reading shows the board is steady; it does not make the number more precise.

Served by the bridge on port 4112. The stock interface is unchanged on port 80 and remains the place to configure pools and upload firmware.

History

Point at a line to read it. The values appear in the legend above each chart, with the time on the right, and a dot marks the sample they came from. Where the series has a gap — a reboot, or the bridge restarting — the readout shows a dash instead of snapping to the nearest reading, and the line breaks for the same reason.

Efficiency

—

There is no power sensor on this board, so the watts have to come from a meter at the wall. Everything else in the row — hashrate, clock, chip temperature, uptime — is captured for you at the moment you save, which is what makes two rows comparable.

Rows recorded inside the warm-up hour are marked with an asterisk. They are real readings of a real machine state, but the hashrate was still climbing, so they cannot be compared against a settled row.

Chips

Chip temperatures are whole degrees and hold one value for minutes at a time, so a reading of 65 means 64.5–65.5. The spread is reliable; a difference of one degree between two chips is not, which is why only the hottest and coldest are marked rather than every chip being shaded along a gradient.
